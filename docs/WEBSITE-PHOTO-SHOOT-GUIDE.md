# Hikari website photo-shoot guide

Use this guide when taking replacement photographs for the public Hikari website. The goal is not a complete menu catalog. It is a small, consistent set of appetite-focused images that feel real, modern and alive.

## The visual target

- Real Hikari food, restaurant surfaces, hands and service moments.
- Food large enough to see rice grains, fish marbling, crisp coating, sauce and steam.
- A mix of scale: one wide establishing photograph, one close food photograph and one true macro detail.
- Soft directional light with natural shadows. Food should look fresh, not flattened by bright overhead light.
- The food provides most of the color. Backgrounds stay quiet and believable.
- Images feel like a moment at Hikari, not an isolated catalog item or stock photograph.

## Minimum shot list

Capture these before trying to photograph the entire menu.

1. **Homepage hero:** one signature roll or composed platter, horizontal, low three-quarter angle. Let the nearest food reach or cross an edge of the frame.
2. **Texture detail:** salmon or sashimi photographed close enough to show marbling and moisture. The full plate does not need to fit.
3. **Crisp texture:** karaage, tempura or gyoza with side light revealing the crust and edges.
4. **Action:** one hand squeezing lemon, drizzling sauce, lifting a piece with chopsticks or a chef finishing a roll. Use a real action that normally happens with that dish.
5. **Table moment:** two or three dishes with hands naturally reaching or serving. Avoid posed smiles toward the camera.
6. **Kitchen moment:** chef plating, slicing or finishing food, with the food remaining visible and important.
7. **Restaurant context:** one calm, inviting view that proves the food belongs at Hikari without making the room the main subject.

For each important subject, capture one horizontal, one vertical and one square-safe version. Do not expect a single photograph to crop perfectly everywhere.

## Phone and camera setup

- Clean the lens before every group of photographs.
- Use the rear camera, not the selfie camera.
- Start with the normal `1x` lens. Use a true optical `2x` lens if available; avoid digital zoom and ultra-wide distortion.
- Turn on the camera grid. Keep plates and table lines intentional.
- Tap the food to focus, then lower exposure slightly if white plates or salmon highlights look blown out.
- Do not use flash, beauty filters, portrait blur or strong automatic food filters.
- Save the original full-resolution files. Do not send the only copy through text messaging or social media compression.
- Take several frames while changing focus slightly. Inspect the food texture at full size before moving on.

## Lighting

- Photograph near a large window with the light coming from the side or slightly behind the food.
- Use a white foam board, menu or clean white surface opposite the window to soften deep shadows.
- Turn off nearby yellow or green overhead lights when they create mixed color on the food.
- Avoid direct noon sun, harsh ceiling spots and flat front lighting.
- Photograph quickly after plating. Fish dries, fried coatings soften and sauces lose their fresh highlights.

## Composition

- Fill roughly 70–90% of the frame with the food for close photographs.
- Move the phone physically closer instead of cropping a distant shot later.
- Let part of the plate or food continue beyond the frame; the entire dish does not always need to be visible.
- Shoot slightly above table level for rolls and fried items so their layers and height remain visible.
- Use a top-down view only when the arrangement itself is important.
- Keep one clear focal point. Secondary dishes and props should support it, not compete.
- Leave usable open space in a few horizontal frames for website text, but also capture a food-filled alternative.
- Vary the framing. Do not center every plate at the same distance against the same background.

## Styling the scene

- Use real Hikari tables, ceramics, napkins and serving pieces.
- Keep backgrounds quiet, clean and slightly imperfect rather than artificial.
- Include only a few believable supporting objects: a cup, sauce dish, chopsticks or another plate.
- Clean plate rims and remove accidental drips, crumbs or wilted garnish.
- Preserve the dish exactly as guests receive it. Do not add garnish or ingredients solely for the photograph.
- Avoid isolated food cutouts, fake steam, neon lighting, artificial bokeh circles and crowded prop arrangements.

## Four-frame recipe for every selected dish

1. **Context:** full dish with enough table to understand the setting.
2. **Close:** food fills most of the frame; part of the plate may be cropped.
3. **Macro:** one important texture fills the frame—fish grain, rice, sauce, crisp coating or dumpling fold.
4. **Action:** a real finishing or eating interaction, captured without blocking the food.

This four-frame sequence gives the website designer genuine choices instead of forcing one catalog photograph into every layout.

## File handling

- Keep original files untouched in a dated folder.
- Name files with the verified dish and shot type, for example `flares-of-hikari_close_horizontal_01`.
- Mark the best frame but keep nearby alternatives.
- Record the exact dish name while shooting; do not rely on visual identification later.
- Do not use AI-edited concept images as customer-facing menu proof. They can guide composition, but final public photographs should accurately show the dish sold at Hikari.

## Before ending the shoot

- Confirm focus at full size, especially on the nearest food.
- Confirm one usable horizontal and one usable vertical frame for each priority subject.
- Check that salmon highlights retain detail and white rice is not overexposed.
- Check that hands look natural and do not cover the food.
- Capture one safer version and one bolder, tighter version of every important setup.
- Back up the original files before editing or sharing them.
